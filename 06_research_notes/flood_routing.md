## Key question 1 — Which studies route around floods using real-time, crowdsourced, sensor or model-based flood information, and which treat flood state as uncertain or probabilistic?

### Takeaway
Most flood-routing papers take road state from a **deterministic** hydrodynamic or stormwater model, or from a single sensing product, then delete or slow edges and run Dijkstra or A*. A few predict a **probability** that a road is flooded, from crowd and traffic data or Bayesian networks, but they stop at prediction and do not route. Others route over **expected values** of probabilistic link failure. I found no peer-reviewed flood-routing system that routes on a confidence bound tied to how much evidence supports each edge.

### Cited Findings
**Model-based (deterministic) flood state → routing**
- de Faria et al. 2026 use a stormwater model to estimate street-level inundation for Hurricanes Florence, Matthew and Dorian (Wilmington, NC). An optimization model applies flood-versus-speed reduction functions over time. Storm-specific flood estimates gave different critical roads than FEMA 100-year maps — [de Faria et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1061/JITSE4.ISENG-2788)
- Panakkal et al. 2023 couple real-time rainfall, a physics-based flood model, and network and spatial analysis to sense real-time flood impact on roads. Outputs are vehicle-class-specific, including minor and residential streets, and identify regions cut off from hospitals; the framework is open source — [Panakkal et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhydrol.2023.130100)
- Jao et al. 2025 (FLOAT) formulate time-dependent flood-aware evacuation as an arc routing problem. Edge cost uses λ (travel time vs flood risk), α (flood-speed penalty) and β (one-way override). On Marikina City (4,892 edges) with simulated depths every minute, 20,000 scenarios show reduced flood exposure at a travel-time cost. High λ and α sometimes produced suboptimal routes "due to the algorithm's local and greedy decision-making" — [Jao et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICNSC66229.2025.00052)
- Lee et al. 2020 combine a NARX neural-network flood prediction with GIS walking-hazard classes to designate time-varying evacuation routes in Seoul — [Lee et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/w12082271)
- He et al. 2021 (CADRO) combine hydrodynamics, topography and human response time in a cellular-automata dynamic evacuation route optimizer (Yangzhou). Compared with A*, it shortens the trapped rate by about 32.70% and mean route length by 34.04% — [He et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compenvurbsys.2021.101622)
- Li et al. 2021 couple a 2D hydrodynamic model with a cellular-automata shortest-route algorithm for pedestrians, using human-instability hazard criteria (Morpeth 2008 flood) — [Li et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11069-021-04960-x)
- Pyatkova et al. 2018 dynamically couple MIKE FLOOD with SUMO microsimulation. Inundated links are halted or delayed by extent, propagation and depth, and trips are cancelled or rerouted — [Pyatkova et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-33616-9_8)
- Singh et al. 2018 link meteorology, land use, a hydrodynamic model and a depth–safe-speed function to assess road vulnerability in an Indian city. More than 40% of road length becomes "immovable" in a 1-in-100-year event — [Singh et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijdrr.2018.03.017). On the city: the abstract names none; a full-text copy on academia.edu describes the Bangalore Urban district and says the Pregnolato curve was "modified accordingly for Indian context" ([academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/111166196/Vulnerability_assessment_of_urban_road_network_from_urban_flood)). A separate TØI document by Prasoon Singh is a Mumbai study ([TØI PDF](https://www.toi.no/getfile.php/1348346-1530708133/Publikasjoner/Flooding%20Mumbai.pdf)), so do not call the IJDRR paper "Mumbai".

**Crowdsourced, social or sensor flood state → routing**
- Panakkal & Padgett 2024 fuse physical, social and visual sensors with physics-based models, using source-specific processing, data fusion and augmentation, to infer link- and network-level flood impacts (Houston case) — [Panakkal & Padgett 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ress.2024.110368)
- Fu et al. 2026 classify social-media posts (BERT-TextCNN filters about 69% of irrelevant content) and extract locations (BERT-BiLSTM-CRF, F1 95.8%). A "multiplicative smooth-decay impedance model" turns discrete disaster events into a continuous spatial risk field for hazard-aware routing — [Fu et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/tgis.70316)
- Alizadeh et al. 2021 estimate floodwater depth from crowdsourced photos of submerged stop signs paired with pre-flood photos, at 4.69 in RMSE on six Harvey pairs. A Gaussian distance-decay function produces an inundation map, and A* finds flood-free paths. The authors note a per-vehicle depth threshold could be set — [Alizadeh et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.11712)
- Yang et al. 2026 (Social-VFG) infer road inundation from vehicle wheels in Weibo images using a wheel detector, SAM and a random forest. Maps agree spatially with GF-3 SAR during the 2023 Zhuozhou flood and are proposed for flood-aware route planning — [Yang et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/17538947.2026.2657148)
- Karanjit et al. 2026 (HAC) forecast gauge height with ML, map inundation with HAND, validate with X/Twitter posts via BERT, then reroute through the GraphHopper API by picking the fastest alternative route outside the inundation area (Lowcountry, SC) — [Karanjit et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envsoft.2026.106958); abstract and method read in the [NHESS-D preprint](https://nhess.copernicus.org/preprints/nhess-2024-25/)
- Songchon et al. 2021 score crowdsourced Twitter flood data quality with logistic regression and fuzzy logic (Phetchaburi, 2016–2018). Fuzzy logic performs better but is more subjective — [Songchon et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compenvurbsys.2021.101690)
- Esparza et al. 2024 show that clusters of 3-1-1 flood reports can supplement physical sensors in a graph observability analysis, reducing needed sensors by 32% (Harris County) — [Esparza et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44212-024-00055-7)

**Probabilistic or uncertain flood state**
- Yuan et al. 2023 predict road inundation probability from topographic, hydrologic and precipitation features. AUC was 0.860 (RF) and 0.810 (AdaBoost) for Harvey, and 0.790 and 0.720 for Imelda. Labels come from INRIX null-speed segments (Harvey) and Waze flood reports (Imelda) — [Yuan et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43762-023-00082-1)
- Yuan et al. 2022 forecast segment flooding 2–4 h ahead with spatio-temporal GCNs on INRIX speeds, with precision above 98% and recall above 96% (Harvey) — [Yuan et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compenvurbsys.2022.101870)
- Fan et al. 2020 model flood spread and recession on roads as an SEIR-like contagion with percolation, where a segment's flood probability depends on flooded neighbours. Precision and recall reach 90% at most time intervals (Harvey) — [Fan et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-70524-x)
- Dong et al. 2020 build a Bayesian network for probabilistic road inundation from cascading channel overflow (Harris County) — [Dong et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scs.2020.102398)
- Toathom & Champrasert 2024 plan relief vehicle routes under flood uncertainty. Uncertainty is "the likelihood that each section of the road will flood", and route fitness is an expected value of waiting time and flood risk — [Toathom & Champrasert 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/app14114482)
- Bucar & Hayeri 2022 derive link-level flood risk from elevation, slope, precipitation, imperviousness and drain-outlet distance (Hoboken). They train an RL route-assistance model and benchmark it against shortest-path and most-reliable-path algorithms — [Bucar & Hayeri 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ress.2021.108202)
- Liu & Meidani 2025 approximate single-source shortest distances with GNNs and then recover paths. Their case study uses SLOSH maximum-of-MEOWs inundation with a 1 ft threshold for evacuation delays, reporting MAPE of 2.11% (Manhattan) and 1.38% (Hillsborough) — [Liu & Meidani 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.09803)
- He et al. 2024 and He et al. 2026 compute per-vehicle-type (car vs SUV/emergency) flood roadworthiness and probability of stability loss on Bristol roads, then assess functionality with isochrones — [He et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trd.2024.104354); [He et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11069-026-08005-z)

**Foundational: routing when edge passability is unknown**
- Polychronopoulos & Tsitsiklis 1996 treat shortest paths with random arc costs where "information on arc cost values is accumulated as the graph is being traversed". They seek a minimal-expected-cost policy and give DP algorithms and negative complexity results — [Polychronopoulos & Tsitsiklis 1996](https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1097-0037(199603)27:2%3C133::AID-NET5%3E3.0.CO;2-L)
- Eyerich et al. 2010 describe the stochastic Canadian Traveler's Problem as one "where adverse weather can cause some roads to be untraversable". The agent knows a weather distribution and observes incident roads. Approaches that "take uncertainty into account explicitly" produce "policies of much higher quality than the optimistic one" — [Eyerich et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v24i1.7542)
- Papadimitriou & Yannakakis 1991 is the standard origin citation for the Canadian Traveller Problem (metadata verified; abstract not read) — [Papadimitriou & Yannakakis 1991](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3975(91)90263-2)

### Inferences
- The field splits into a **hydro/physics route** (accurate depths, but needs DEM, drainage and forcing, and is usually server- or desktop-bound) and a **sensing/crowd route** (cheap, but sparse and noisy). Only Panakkal & Padgett 2024, Karanjit et al. 2026 and Fu et al. 2026 fuse sources and then route. None of the three exposes per-edge evidence counts or confidence to the routing cost.
- The probabilistic studies (Yuan 2022/2023, Fan 2020, Dong 2020) are **predictors**, not routers. CityPulse can cite them as candidate sources of p̄ and as evaluation precedents: Yuan 2023 reports AUC 0.72–0.86, a useful yardstick against CityPulse's AUROC of 0.609 on a proxy label.
- Yuan et al. 2023 treat **INRIX null speed as "flooded"**, the mirror image of the council's "traversal-silence" idea: there, the absence of traffic is positive evidence of flooding. Itoi et al. 2017 (see KQ3) infers blocked segments from users deviating from recommended routes. Together they are the closest published precedents for learning passability from traversal behaviour rather than from explicit reports.
- The CTP literature (Eyerich 2010) supplies a citable argument that explicit uncertainty handling beats optimism when roads may be untraversable. CityPulse's pessimistic bound is a heuristic in that spirit, but not a CTP policy: it does not plan to observe and re-route. The paper should place it relative to CTP and recourse models.

### Gaps
- I found no flood-routing paper that weights crowd reports by source and **decays them by age** before routing. Fu et al. 2026 decay over space, not time; Songchon 2021 scores quality but does not route. Confirm with the `crowd_flood_sensing.md` notes.
- I found no flood-routing paper that **calibrates** its edge probabilities (Brier score or ECE) before routing. Yuan 2023 reports AUC only.

## Key question 2 — Which emergency/EMS/ambulance routing studies under flooding exist, and which use depth-disruption functions?

### Takeaway
EMS work under flooding is mostly **accessibility and coverage** analysis: isochrones and compliance with statutory response times. It uses inundation thresholds (for example 25 cm, 10/25/45 cm, or 1 ft) or vehicle-stability curves rather than turn-by-turn navigation. Lupa et al. 2026 (Ambulance STARS) is the closest to an operational EMS router, and it is cloud-based. Depth–speed functions appear in de Faria 2026, Singh 2018 (an adapted Pregnolato curve), Jao 2025 (an α penalty) and Dong N. 2020 (unverified). Pregnolato 2017, Green 2017 and Coles 2017 are already in refs.tsv.

### Cited Findings
- Lupa et al. 2026 derive flood extent from Sentinel-1 SAR change detection into road passability layers. An in-memory routing graph uses ambulance speed models from four years of EMS GPS (61.8 km/h with signals, 37.2 km/h without). On the September 2024 Nysa flood, SAR marked 8.5 km of a 656.7 km network impassable and the optical mask 17.3 km. Because the two products "captured different phases of the flood wave", they were used "to bracket operational uncertainty rather than to define a single ground truth". The 15-min reachable share fell from 88% to 80% (SAR) or 39.8% (optical) — [Lupa et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs18173004)
- Yu et al. 2020 model the coverage of all Ambulance and Fire & Rescue stations in England under floods of varying severity. Even low-magnitude floods reduce national compliance with mandatory response times — [Yu et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41893-020-0516-7)
- Tahvildari et al. 2022 couple storm-surge and 2D rainfall models (Hurricane Irene, SE Virginia) with an ambulance-positioning optimization under 10, 25 and 45 cm access thresholds. No traffic analysis zone reached the trauma centre within the golden hour at 10 or 25 cm; after adding stations or staging, 52% and 75% did — [Tahvildari et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1061/(ASCE)NH.1527-6996.0000524)
- He et al. 2026 compute the probability of stability loss for SUVs/emergency vehicles vs cars and map emergency coverage from all Bristol hospitals with isochrones. SUVs/emergency vehicles face lower network flood risk than cars — [He et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11069-026-08005-z)
- Chen et al. 2025 simulate EMS performance in Zhengzhou (normal, 1-in-50, 1-in-100, and the "7.20" 2021 disaster). Resource shortages are a key delay factor, and ambulance sharing raises 10-min and 30-min coverage by 15.2% and 22.7% — [Chen et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44172-025-00481-8)
- Song et al. 2025 schedule water-ground multimodal rescue with an improved A* under the actual road inundation situation — [Song et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/risa.17450)
- Alabbad et al. 2021 compute shortest paths to critical amenities (hospitals and others) under 100- and 500-year floods in Iowa. Edge losses reach 18%, and the nearest amenity can "flip to the second or higher-orders" — [Alabbad et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2021.148476)
- Loreti et al. 2022 argue that percolation and giant-component measures are inadequate on a realistic high-resolution flood simulation. They partition the network by accessibility to local towns that provide critical services — [Loreti et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-04927-3)
- Depth–speed functions: de Faria 2026 uses "flood versus speed reduction functions" ([link](https://doi.org/10.1061/JITSE4.ISENG-2788)); Singh 2018 uses a "safety speed function" relating depth to speed reduction ([link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijdrr.2018.03.017)); Jao 2025 uses an α flood-speed penalty ([link](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICNSC66229.2025.00052)).

### Inferences
- The EMS literature measures **coverage loss**; it does not produce an explained, per-trip route under uncertain passability. CityPulse's "emergency z≈2" class sits in an empty cell: it would be an EMS-specific risk-averse router. It needs a different evaluation, such as statutory-time compliance (Yu 2020, Coles 2017) rather than detour percentage.
- Tahvildari 2022 and He 2026 justify **class-specific depth thresholds**: emergency vehicles tolerate more depth than cars. This supports CityPulse's chance constraint, but CityPulse should cite actual thresholds; Tahvildari 2022 is one usable range (10/25/45 cm).
- Lupa 2026's finding that two satellite products disagree strongly on the rare impassable class gives direct evidence that "ground truth" for passability is itself uncertain. CityPulse can cite it to motivate a pessimistic bound.

### Gaps
- No EMS-under-flood study I found uses crowdsourced reports or on-device routing. No India-specific EMS-under-flood routing study was found; check `chennai_india.md`.

## Key question 3 — Are there systems that work offline or on-device, or that explain routing decisions?

### Takeaway
I found **one peer-reviewed offline mobile evacuation-routing app** (Itoi et al. 2017, generic outdoor evacuation, not flood-specific) and no peer-reviewed on-device *flood* router for a city-scale graph. "Explanation" in the flood-routing literature means map visualisation in web or digital-twin dashboards, not natural-language justification of a route. Hobby and hackathon projects (2025–2026) now bundle on-device LLMs with offline shelter routing, but none are peer-reviewed.

### Cited Findings
- Itoi et al. 2017 design an offline mobile evacuation-guidance app for when "communication infrastructures" are damaged. Map matching corrects noisy GPS. The app "automatically estimate[s] a blocked road segment based on the difference between recommended route and actual evacuation route", recomputes a route avoiding it, and can share discovered blocked segments — [Itoi et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41070-017-0013-1); the method text was read in the [authors' PDF](https://www2.itc.kansai-u.ac.jp/~sasabe/publications/itoi17OfflineMobileApplication.pdf)
- Alabbad et al. 2024 is a web application for non-experts: road status, shortest paths to amenities, point-to-point routing, location-allocation and service areas. It links USGS gauge stages and NWS HEFS **10-day river-level probabilities** to precomputed inundation extents (60 for Cedar Rapids, 46 for Charles City) to mark closed edges. The authors note Google Maps "has limited functionality for routing during flooding" — [Alabbad et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44212-024-00040-0)
- Kaynak et al. 2025 present a city-scale digital twin (Waterloo, Iowa) that integrates flood forecasting, transportation networks and critical infrastructure for interactive 3D simulation of mobility disruption — [Kaynak et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.uclim.2025.102640)
- Mandal et al. 2026 (FlowsDT, Galveston) build a 1D-2D PCSWMM-based geospatial digital twin, validated with historical events and social sensing. Road inundation above 0.3 m rises 6.7% from 2- to 100-year storms — [Mandal et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compenvurbsys.2026.102414)
- Panakkal et al. 2023 stress open-source tools and interoperability with existing situational-awareness tools — [Panakkal et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhydrol.2023.130100)
- Non-peer-reviewed on-device prior art: "Nongor" (Bangladesh) claims offline flood-avoiding shelter routing (Dijkstra over OSM), signed phone-to-phone SOS and Gemma on the handset ([GitHub](https://github.com/AuvroIslam/Nongor)). "LIKAS" (Philippines) claims "Zero network calls at runtime", a bundled pedestrian routing graph and a fine-tuned Gemma model ([GitHub](https://github.com/JpCurada/likas)).

### Inferences
- CityPulse's combination of offline operation, a city-scale graph (193k nodes), per-edge belief, and verifier-gated NL explanation has no peer-reviewed counterpart in what I found. The novelty claim should be phrased as this **combination**, not as "first offline flood router". Itoi 2017 is offline routing with learned blocked segments, and hackathon projects already pair Gemma with offline flood routing.
- Itoi 2017's "deviation reveals blocked segment" mechanism is close prior art for the council's traversal-silence idea and should be cited.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study that evaluates natural-language explanations of flood routes, or LLM explanations of any hazard route, with users. The Tehran explainable-routing DT-SDSS (Rezaei et al. 2026) is UNVERIFIED and not flood-specific.

## Key question 4 — Any 2024–2026 work combining flood forecasts (e.g., Google Flood Hub, GloFAS) or digital twins with navigation?

### Takeaway
I found **no** peer-reviewed paper that feeds Google Flood Hub or GloFAS into street-level routing. Forecast-to-routing links in 2024–2026 use national or regional forecasts: NWS HEFS probabilities (Alabbad 2024) and ML gauge forecasts with HAND (Karanjit 2026). Digital twins (Kaynak 2025, Mandal 2026) model road disruption but do not ship a navigation product.

### Cited Findings
- Alabbad et al. 2024 tie 10-day HEFS river-level probabilities to closed-edge maps in a routing web app — [Alabbad et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44212-024-00040-0)
- Karanjit et al. 2026 forecast gauge height (LSTM), map inundation with HAND, and reroute via GraphHopper — [Karanjit et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envsoft.2026.106958); method from the [preprint](https://nhess.copernicus.org/preprints/nhess-2024-25/)
- Kaynak et al. 2025 and Mandal et al. 2026 are flood digital twins with transportation-disruption analysis — [Kaynak et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.uclim.2025.102640); [Mandal et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compenvurbsys.2026.102414)
- Context only (not a routing paper): a 2025 J. Flood Risk Management study comparing GloFAS v4 and Google Flood Hub in Mali notes their "inability to fully capture the process of flooding, especially in urban settings" and recommends "a more spatially explicit flood forecasting approach" — [Ovid record, JFRM 10.1111/jfr3.70247](https://www.ovid.com/journals/jfrm/fulltext/10.1111/jfr3.70247~assessing-the-riverine-flood-forecast-skill-of-glofas-and) (not Crossref-verified here; authors per the EGU abstract: Kuipers, Oldenburg, Sutanudjaja, Phung, Ficchì, van den Homberg — [EGU25-302](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU25/EGU25-302.html?pdf=))

### Inferences
- Riverine forecast systems such as Flood Hub and GloFAS are point- or reach-scale, while Chennai road flooding is largely pluvial and drainage-driven. The Mali study's remark about weak urban capture supports treating Flood Hub as at most a coarse prior or alert trigger in CityPulse, not an edge-level signal.

### Gaps
- No evidence found of any study (2024–2026) using Google Flood Hub or GloFAS for road navigation. This may be a genuine gap or a search-coverage limit, since Consensus was exhausted after 6 queries.

## Per-paper summaries (abstract-level)

1. **de Faria et al. 2026.** Stormwater-model street inundation for three hurricanes feeds a time-varying routing optimization with flood–speed reduction functions. Arcs are removed or slowed over time, and storm-specific results are compared with FEMA 100-year maps; storm-specific estimates matter for identifying critical roads.
2. **Bucar & Hayeri 2022.** Data-driven link flood-risk model for Hoboken (elevation, slope, precipitation, imperviousness, distance to drain outlets). An RL route-assistance model trained on these risks is benchmarked against shortest and most-reliable paths and gives a balance of length and reliability; link outputs are offered for planning closures.
3. **Lupa et al. 2026 (Ambulance STARS).** Sentinel-1 SAR change detection produces road passability layers, routed with empirical ambulance speeds from four years of GPS. Tested on the Sept 2024 Nysa flood: SAR and optical masks disagreed strongly on impassable roads, so they were used to bracket uncertainty. Accessibility maps computed in 12–34 s.
4. **Jao et al. 2025 (FLOAT).** Time-dependent arc-routing evacuation with λ, α and β cost parameters on Marikina City and per-minute simulated depths. 20,000 scenarios show less flood exposure than shortest-path routing at a travel-time cost; the greedy local structure can yield suboptimal routes.
5. **Panakkal et al. 2023.** Open-source framework coupling real-time rainfall and a physics-based flood model with network analysis for vehicle-class-specific road conditions in Houston, including minor streets. Also identifies loss of access to hospitals.
6. **Panakkal & Padgett 2024.** Fuses physical, social and visual sensors with physics models through source-specific processing, fusion and augmentation to infer link- and network-level flood impacts. A limited Houston case suggests repurposing existing data improves roadway situational awareness.
7. **Yang et al. 2026 (Social-VFG).** Wheel-contour indicator (detector plus SAM) and a random forest classify road inundation severity from Weibo images. Maps agree spatially with GF-3 SAR in the 2023 Zhuozhou flood and are proposed for flood-aware route planning.
8. **Tahvildari et al. 2022.** Serially coupled surge and rainfall models quantify compound flooding of roads (Hurricane Irene). An ambulance-positioning optimization under 10/25/45 cm thresholds shows golden-hour access collapses without added stations or staging.
9. **Chen et al. 2025.** Simulation framework combining inundation, EMS facilities and population-weighted demand (Zhengzhou). Shows supply-demand mismatch dominates EMS deterioration; ambulance sharing beats limited fleet additions.
10. **Yu et al. 2020.** National-scale (England) modelling of ambulance and fire-station coverage under floods of varying severity. Even low-magnitude floods reduce compliance with mandatory response times, linked to centralisation policies.
11. **Song et al. 2025.** Four-level water-ground multimodal emergency transport network with random transshipment points from the actual inundation situation. Hierarchical optimization uses an improved A* and a greedy scheduling algorithm.
12. **Lee et al. 2020.** NARX neural-network flood prediction plus GIS walking-hazard classification designates spatiotemporally varying evacuation routes in a flood-prone part of Seoul.
13. **Li C. et al. 2025.** Review of urban flood evacuation planning: flood simulation, hazard assessment, shelters, route algorithms and evacuee movement. Notes that metaheuristics perform well and points to speeds, psychology, multimodal evacuation and power lines as future directions.
14. **He M. et al. 2021 (CADRO).** Cellular-automata dynamic evacuation route optimizer incorporating hydrodynamics, topography and response time. Beats A* on trapped rate and route length in Yangzhou.
15. **Li B. et al. 2021.** Coupled 2D hydrodynamic and CA shortest-route planner for pedestrians using human-instability criteria (Morpeth 2008). Mechanics-based criteria identify extremes better than empirical ones.
16. **Fu et al. 2026.** NLP pipeline over social media (topic filtering, location NER) plus a smooth-decay impedance field over events for hazard-aware SSSP routing with STR indexing on large networks.
17. **Wang et al. 2019.** Flood-aware failure model for road networks (covers 90.6% of Harvey road closures) shows a discontinuous phase transition: small local floods can cause large-scale abrupt failure.
18. **Dong et al. 2020.** Bayesian network linking channel overflow to road inundation (Harris County) gives probabilistic road-failure risk under cascading flooding.
19. **Alabbad et al. 2021.** Graph-theoretic accessibility analysis to critical amenities under 100- and 500-year floods across Iowa communities. Up to 18% edge loss; many bridges become inaccessible.
20. **Alabbad et al. 2024.** Public web decision-support tool for flood routing, amenity access, location-allocation and service areas. Links gauge readings and 10-day HEFS probabilities to precomputed inundation extents.
21. **Kaynak et al. 2025.** City-scale flood digital twin integrating forecasts, transport and critical infrastructure for interactive 3D impact simulation (Waterloo, Iowa).
22. **Mandal et al. 2026 (FlowsDT).** PCSWMM 1D-2D geospatial digital twin for Galveston, validated with history and social sensing. Quantifies building and road inundation across return periods.
23. **Yuan et al. 2022.** STGCN models on INRIX speed data predict road flooding status 2–4 h ahead (Harvey); an elevation-aware adjacency is tested.
24. **Yuan et al. 2023.** RF and AdaBoost predict road inundation probability from topographic, hydrologic and precipitation features. Labels come from Waze reports (Imelda) and INRIX null speeds (Harvey); AUC 0.72–0.86.
25. **Esparza et al. 2024.** Cross-correlation of 3-1-1 reports with sensor water levels plus graph observability shows crowd reports can replace about 32% of the additional physical sensors needed.
26. **Fan et al. 2020.** SEIR-like flood contagion with network percolation predicts spread and recession of road flooding (Harvey) with about 90% precision and recall at most intervals.
27. **Songchon et al. 2021.** Logistic regression and fuzzy logic estimate the quality of flood tweets (Phetchaburi), allowing crowdsourced uncertainty to be quantified.
28. **He et al. 2024.** Combines flood hazard, vehicle vulnerability (car vs SUV severity factor) and topology measures to assess Bristol road-network functionality across return periods.
29. **He et al. 2026.** Probabilistic stability-loss assessment per vehicle type turned into road flood-risk maps and EMS isochrones from Bristol hospitals; emergency vehicles are less affected than cars.
30. **Loreti et al. 2022.** On high-resolution flood simulation, percolation and giant-component measures are inadequate; the paper proposes accessibility partitions to local towns and critical services.
31. **Singh et al. 2018.** Hydrodynamic modelling of 1-in-10 and 1-in-100-year rainfall plus a depth–safe-speed curve gives an Indian urban road vulnerability index. Over 40% of road length becomes immovable at 1-in-100. Full text indicates Bangalore and a Pregnolato-curve adaptation.
32. **Morelli & Cunha 2021.** "Efficiency of alternative" vulnerability metric needing only network topology and an OD matrix, applied to floods in São Carlos (Brazil). Active modes and shorter trips are more robust.
33. **Lalwani et al. 2025.** Microscopic traffic simulation of Dadar, Mumbai (5,159 links, 2,147 nodes, 18 zones) tests disruption scenarios and evacuation strategies. An Urban Mobility India conference deck describing the same network size (5,159 links, 2,147 nodes, 18 zones) represents waterlogging as a 50% speed cut or as disconnection of roads within 300 m of frequent waterlogging points ([UMI PDF](https://www.urbanmobilityindia.in/Upload/Conference/f1f9f1d3-891a-4b2c-a565-9ef0732750da.pdf)). It is not verified as the same authors' work.
34. **Pyatkova et al. 2018.** Dynamic MIKE FLOOD and SUMO coupling estimates flood impacts on traffic (cancelled and rerouted trips, lost hours, fuel and CO2).
35. **Toathom & Champrasert 2024.** GA-based relief VRP whose enhanced population uses expected route performance under per-section flood likelihood; evaluated on CVRP benchmarks with simulated flood uncertainty.
36. **Karanjit et al. 2026.** Human-AI convergence system: LSTM gauge forecasts, HAND inundation, BERT-filtered X posts and GraphHopper alternative-route selection outside inundated areas (South Carolina Lowcountry).
37. **Itoi et al. 2017.** Offline mobile evacuation-guidance app with map matching. It infers blocked segments from deviations between recommended and actual paths, reroutes, and can share the findings.
38. **Alizadeh et al. 2021.** Crowd photos of stop signs give floodwater depth, a Gaussian distance-decay function interpolates an inundation map, and A* finds flood-free routes; 4.69 in RMSE on six Harvey photo pairs.
39. **Liu & Meidani 2025.** GNN surrogate for single-source shortest distances and paths under topology changes, applied to SLOSH-based hurricane flooding evacuation delays to shelters.
40. **Papadimitriou & Yannakakis 1991.** Title-level: origin of the Canadian Traveller Problem (shortest paths when blocked edges are discovered only on arrival). Abstract not read.
41. **Polychronopoulos & Tsitsiklis 1996.** Stochastic shortest path with recourse: arc costs are revealed during traversal, and the goal is a minimal expected-cost policy. DP algorithms, complexity bounds and heuristics.
42. **Eyerich et al. 2010.** Stochastic CTP with a known weather distribution and local observation. Uncertainty-aware policies clearly beat the common optimistic policy, in theory and in experiments.

## Gaps the literature leaves

1. **Evidence-count-aware risk bounds in routing.** None of the flood-routing papers found makes edge cost depend on how much evidence supports an edge's flood estimate (n_eff); the closest are expected-value (Toathom 2024) and scenario-bracketing (Lupa 2026) approaches.
2. **Time decay and source weighting of crowd reports inside the router.** Papers either score report quality offline (Songchon 2021), spatially decay events (Fu 2026), or treat reports as labels for prediction (Yuan 2023). None decays report weight with age before routing.
3. **Calibration of road-flood probabilities.** Prediction papers report AUC, precision and recall only (Yuan 2022/2023; Fan 2020). No calibration (Brier, ECE) was found in the flood-routing literature.
4. **Offline, on-device, city-scale flood routing.** Only Itoi 2017 (generic evacuation, offline) was found peer-reviewed. Existing flood routers are web-, cloud- or digital-twin-based (Alabbad 2024; Lupa 2026; Kaynak 2025; Karanjit 2026). Hackathon projects show the idea is in circulation but has not been evaluated.
5. **Faithful natural-language explanations of flood routes.** No peer-reviewed flood-routing work found generates or verifies textual route explanations; "explanation" means maps and dashboards.
6. **Forecast → street-level routing with Flood Hub or GloFAS.** None found. A 2025 GloFAS/Flood Hub evaluation notes weak urban capture (JFRM, see KQ4).
7. **Indian and Chennai coverage.** Indian work is vulnerability or simulation (Singh 2018, apparently Bangalore; Lalwani 2025, Mumbai). No Chennai flood-routing study was found here; defer to `chennai_india.md`.
8. **Ground truth for passability.** Lupa 2026 shows strong disagreement between SAR and optical impassability. Yuan 2023 uses null traffic speed as a label. No benchmark dataset of time-stamped street passability under flooding was found.
9. **EMS navigation (not coverage) under uncertain flooding.** EMS work measures coverage loss (Yu 2020; Tahvildari 2022; He 2026; Chen 2025). A per-trip, risk-bounded EMS router with explanations is absent.
10. **Learning passability from traversal (negative evidence).** Only indirect precedents exist: Itoi 2017 (deviation reveals blockage) and the INRIX null-speed-as-flooded labels in Yuan 2023 and Fan 2020. No study treats vehicles passing without reporting as evidence that a road is passable for flood routing.
